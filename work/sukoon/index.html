INSIGHTS & GAP

Most mental health apps ask you to talk first. Most men want to handle it first.

The apps I reviewed were built for everyone, and most offered either therapy or peer support, rarely both. My survey pointed the other way: 12 of 19 men said they should manage on their own, yet 16 of 19 would try help that stays confidential. Nothing I found gave men a private first step before any conversation.

We’ve made men’s mental health about talking.

But what a man really needs first…

…is a way to start on his own.

09 · The insight

It Wasn’t Mainly Stigma. i was “I Should handle this myself.”

So the problem changed. Men weren't refusing help. They were refusing to ask for it. A product that opens with "talk to someone" asks for the very thing they avoid. One that lets them start alone, privately and on their own terms, fits how they already cope.

Three principles for the app

01

start alone.

The first things you can do are private: check in on your mood, or take a self-assessment. No people, no profile, no posting.

02

name it first.

The app helps you find a word for the feeling. Sharing it is a choice you make afterwards, and it can stay anonymous.

03

offer, don't push.

After a self-assessment, the app suggests a support group or a professional. You decide whether to go.

How might we help young men who believe they should handle it alone take a private first step toward support, without asking them to talk first?

17 · Three ways in

One initiative, three ways in.

I designed a mental health awareness and support initiative for men. It uses a digital experience, local-language printed material and a physical wellbeing kit. One format would not suit everyone, so each route meets a different situation: a phone, a wall, or a desk.

ROUTE 01 · DIGITAL

Sukoon app

WHO IT SERVES

Young adults, 18–35, who are comfortable with smartphones.

WHY THIS FORMAT

The app offers a private, familiar space to reflect on feelings and take a small next step. Young adults can engage at their own pace, making room for wellbeing in everyday life.

ROUTE 02 · PRINT

A4 self-assessment sheets and local-language posters

WHO IT SERVES

People who rarely use apps, or who read more easily in their own language.

WHY THIS FORMAT

Paper needs no data, download or account. A poster can sit where people already wait.

ROUTE 03 · KIT

A box with stickers, A–Z cards, a mindfulness journal and a mood tracker

WHO IT SERVES

Anyone who wants a small daily habit, away from a screen.

WHY THIS FORMAT

Objects on a desk are reminders. Reflection becomes part of the day instead of an app you must open.

19 · Route 2 · Print

Help that doesn't need an app.

The print route is for men who may never download Sukoon: men on a shared phone, men who rarely use apps, and men who read Hindi more easily than English. It has two parts. A poster starts the thought in a place where people already wait. An A4 self-check sheet lets him check on himself in private, with a pen, and keep the result.

What already exists: posters and four self-check sheets.

IS THE A4 FORM A SURVEY?

No. It is a self-assessment.

Each sheet is a short self-check that a man fills in and scores himself: anxiety (built on the GAD-7 structure), depression (a simplified BDI), sleep, and substance use. It is not a research survey and not a diagnosis. Nobody collects it. Because it gives a score, the questions, score ranges and wording need review by a qualified mental health professional before anything is printed.

WHY A CARD, NOT A BOOKLET

It uses the A–Z card size and style, so it sits in the same deck and is the first thing seen when the lid comes off. One side explains, the other side gets him doing something before he can put the box away.

I went in believing stigma was the main barrier, because that's what reports and campaigns say. My own survey pointed somewhere else: most men had struggled, few had asked for help, and the main reason was that they felt they should handle it themselves. That changed what the app opens with. It starts with something you do alone.
